Nigeria Waste Projection Explorer

Scenario-based projections of municipal solid waste by state, 2023 to 2035

Scenarios, not forecasts. The 2023 baseline comes from a state-level estimate (27.5 Mt) that is lower than World Bank-based levels (about 43.5 Mt). Use the baseline scale to see the effect.

What is this page about?

Every day, households and businesses in Nigeria throw away food scraps, plastic, paper and packaging. Experts call this municipal solid waste (MSW). Cities keep growing, so the amount keeps rising, yet there are few exact counts. This page shows how much waste each state is estimated to produce, and how that could change up to 2035, so planners can decide where to put collection, recycling and composting.

It is based on a research project that cleaned the available data, tested forecasting methods on monthly Lagos dumpsite records, and projected waste from population growth and city growth. The numbers are scenarios (“what if” pictures), not exact predictions.

How to use it: choose a scenario and a year, then click any state on the map. The map, the numbers, the chart and the table all update.

What the controls do. Low: each person throws away the same amount as today, so waste grows only because there are more people. Central: each person throws away a little more as cities grow (this setting was fitted to a World Bank projection). High: waste per person grows as fast as the share of people living in cities. The elasticity slider sets how strongly city growth raises waste per person (0 means not at all). The baseline scale adjusts the 2023 starting point: the state estimate (27.5 Mt) is lower than the World Bank-based level (about 43.5 Mt), and a scale of about 1.58 reproduces that higher level.

How to read the map: darker orange means more waste. Colours group the 37 areas into five equal-sized bands, and the legend gives each band in million tonnes (Mt) a year. Hover over a state to see its figure and click it to see its own trend. When you use the Top 10 or Bottom 10 buttons below, the other states fade. States with the most people, such as Lagos and Kano, generate the most waste.

Large states rank high mostly because they have more people.

Plain-language glossary

Municipal solid waste (MSW): the everyday waste from homes and businesses.

Organic waste: food and garden waste. It can be composted or used to make biogas.

Mt (million tonnes): the unit of weight used here. One Mt is one million tonnes.

Scenario: a “what if” path based on stated assumptions. It is not a prediction.

Urbanisation: the growing share of people living in cities.

Baseline: the starting estimate for 2023 from which every projection grows.

Dumpsite: a site where collected waste is taken and left, usually without treatment.

Assumptions and limits
  • Waste(state, year) = 2023 waste × population growth × per-person multiplier, where the multiplier is (urban share ÷ 2023 urban share) to the power of the elasticity.
  • Low sets the elasticity to 0, Central uses a value calibrated to the World Bank 2016 to 2030 projection, and High sets it to 1.0.
  • Organic share falls as waste per person rises, following the pattern across the 37 areas (partly driven by Lagos).
  • Every state grows at the national UN rate, so state differences come from the 2023 baseline only.
  • The baseline level is the main uncertainty. Setting the scale to about 1.58 reproduces the World Bank-based level.

What this shows: the black line is the weight of waste delivered to five Lagos dumpsites each month, in thousand tonnes, from January 2022 to December 2024. The dashed lines are three possible paths for 2025 and 2026, and the shaded area is the range between them: Low (receipts keep falling), Central (the average of the three most accurate methods) and High (receipts stay about where they are). The three boxes give the yearly totals in million tonnes.

Why it falls: about half of the fall comes from one dumpsite, Solous, where truck trips roughly halved from July 2023. That points to a change in how sites are used, not necessarily to Lagos producing less waste.

Not the same as waste produced: this is waste that reached these sites. Lagos is estimated to produce about 3.8 Mt a year, so in 2023 only about 47% of it reached these dumpsites, and about 36% in 2024. The rest may be uncollected, burned, dumped elsewhere or taken to other facilities, and the data cannot tell which.

How the paths were made: seven forecasting methods were tested like a practice exam on past months. The best, a straight line through the last 12 months, was off by 6.4% on average. These are scenarios, not predictions.
